Concept / policy proposal · revised October 2026

People decide.
Machines help.
Memory remains.

The Human Machine Party proposes a democratic model in which people retain political authority, digital tools make participation practical, artificial intelligence helps explain choices, and public cultural memory is preserved as inspectable infrastructure rather than disappearing into inaccessible archives.

Explore the modelSee the archive proposal

This site describes proposals and design questions. It does not claim that a complete voting system, national archive network or AI-governance system is already operating.

01 · Democratic model

A vote should be a voice, not a blank cheque.

HMP proposes a direct/liquid-democracy relationship between citizens and representatives. People could vote themselves, delegate selected topics to trusted people, and use AI as an explanatory tool. The central distinction is between assistance and authority.

Publish the actual question

Legislative text, plain-language explanation, costs, uncertainties and competing arguments are published together.

clarity

Let people examine it

Citizens, experts and machine tools can interrogate evidence, assumptions and consequences before the decision period closes.

deliberation

Vote directly or delegate

A person may decide personally or authorise a trusted delegate for a subject. A direct vote overrides that delegation for that decision.

choice

Translate the result into representation

An elected HMP representative would carry a valid instruction into the relevant institution, subject to applicable constitutional and legal duties.

mandate

Audit what happened

The record should show what was proposed, how the decision was reached, what the representative did, and what outcomes followed.

accountability
A

Flexible delegation

Delegation is intended to reduce the burden of following every issue. It should be revocable, scoped and never create an extra vote.

B

Representatives as fiduciaries of a mandate

The proposal limits personal policy discretion, but does not erase legal duties, emergency judgment or institutional procedure. Those boundaries require explicit rules.

C

Auditable candidate selection

HMP has explored random selection from a pre-declared eligible pool, using independently reproducible randomness. Any implementation would need legal review and anti-manipulation safeguards.

02 · Public memory infrastructure

The archive should know what survived.

A new HMP policy concept is a universal public catalogue for national television, radio and related documentary archives. Public-domain/open material could be directly accessible; protected material could remain catalogued and, where lawful, replicated as encrypted data with access controlled separately.

Layer 1

Universal catalogue

Stable IDs, dates, channels, people, transcript indexes, provenance, rights state and relationships between versions.

Layer 2

Content addressing

Cryptographic hashes identify exact media objects and allow integrity checking, deduplication and reproducible citations.

Layer 3

Distributed preservation

Institutional seeders, WebSeeds and peer protocols can replicate authorised or encrypted archival chunks across many locations.

Layer 4

Browser runtime

WebTorrent-compatible delivery, WebAssembly decoders, WebCodecs, Service Workers and OPFS can support rich in-browser access.

Layer 5

Rights engine

Viewing, download, research, AI evaluation and training permissions are machine-readable and separable from preservation.

Open

Public-domain or openly licensed material: searchable, streamable, downloadable and reusable under its stated terms.

Authorised access

Copyrighted material may be available to defined users or purposes. Encryption can separate wide preservation from permission to decode.

Known but restricted

A catalogue can still record that an object exists, where it came from and why access is restricted. Discovery and permission are different layers.

Important limitation: encryption and peer-to-peer distribution do not themselves resolve copyright, performer rights, donor restrictions, personal egoprivacy, culturally sensitive material or contractual restrictions. A legal framework would have to specify what may be replicated, who may decrypt it, and under what conditions.
03 · AI and public knowledge

Better memory can support better machine reasoning.

A provenance-rich public archive could be useful to AI without assuming that every preserved work should be copied into model training. Retrieval, evaluation, research and training are distinct uses and should have distinct permissions.

Human authority

Intelligence does not automatically confer political authority.

HMP distinguishes competence from legitimacy. A highly capable machine may forecast consequences, translate complex documents and surface overlooked evidence. Those abilities do not, by themselves, grant an independent vote or a right to govern.

“Explain the options. Expose uncertainty. Preserve the sources. Let people retain the final say.”
Grounding and retrievalAn AI can cite a broadcast, transcript, document and timestamp instead of relying only on what its model weights appear to remember.
EvaluationHistorical archives can test whether models distinguish past from present, identify provenance, resist fabrication and handle contested evidence.
Rights-permitted trainingExplicit machine-readable permissions can identify material lawfully available for public, academic or commercial training.
Alignment researchLong-run cultural records can expose models to changing norms, minority perspectives and disagreement—but frequency in an archive must never be mistaken for moral truth.

Machine as ruler

  • Collapses prediction and authority into one institution.
  • Makes value choices difficult to contest.
  • Creates concentrated failure modes.
  • Risks treating optimisation targets as political legitimacy.

Machine as adviser

  • Provides analysis, translation, simulation and evidence retrieval.
  • Displays uncertainty and competing interpretations.
  • Can be audited against underlying sources.
  • Remains subject to revocation, law and human institutions.
04 · Safeguards

More participation requires stronger constraints.

Digital participation is not trustworthy because it is online, decentralised or AI-assisted. Any real system would need rules that can survive adversarial scrutiny and remain usable by people with different abilities, resources and levels of technical confidence.

01

Verified eligibility

Define who may vote and prevent duplicate participation while collecting no more identity data than necessary.

02

Ballot secrecy / egoprivacy

HMP uses egoprivacy for personal informational privacy. Public accountability for institutions should not require exposing individual political choices to coercion or retaliation.

03

Independent verification

Counting procedures, software, question wording and security assumptions need independent review and meaningful challenge mechanisms.

04

Accessibility

Digital-first cannot mean digital-only. Participation must account for disability, language, connectivity, literacy and assisted-access requirements.

05

Rights and minorities

Majoritarian decisions operate inside constitutional and human-rights constraints rather than erasing them.

06

Source transparency

AI summaries should link back to evidence and show uncertainty, provenance and disagreement instead of presenting a single synthetic answer as unquestionable.

07

Institutional fallback

Offline procedures, recounts, incident response and legal remedies remain necessary even when the normal interface is digital.

08

Separation of powers

No single model provider, identity service, archive operator or party administrator should silently control the entire decision pipeline.

05 · Open questions

A serious proposal should publish its unresolved problems.

The following issues are not implementation details to be hidden later. They determine whether a digital democratic model would be legitimate, lawful and resilient.

Who exactly constitutes the electorate for each decision?

A party membership ballot, a constituency electorate and a national electorate are different things. Eligibility rules, participation thresholds and abstention treatment must be fixed before a result can become an instruction.

What happens when participation is very low?

Possible approaches include quorum rules, confidence thresholds, default delegation, deferral or representative discretion. Each creates different incentives and should be evaluated empirically.

How would emergencies work?

A parliament or council sometimes must act faster than a public deliberation cycle. Any emergency mandate would need narrow scope, time limits, publication requirements and retrospective review.

Can a representative always be legally bound by a digital instruction?

Not necessarily. Constitutional systems differ, and elected officials may retain duties or discretion that a private party agreement cannot lawfully override. The model must adapt to the jurisdiction rather than pretend those constraints do not exist.

Could AI manipulation dominate deliberation?

Yes. Systems would need provenance, model diversity, adversarial testing, disclosure of synthetic content where appropriate, source access and the ability to deliberate without a machine intermediary.

Does a distributed archive mean everything becomes public?

No. Catalogue visibility, preservation, access and reuse should be separate. Some records can be known to exist while remaining restricted; others may be replicated only as encrypted data; some should not be distributed at all.

Should archived media be used for AI training?

Only when the relevant legal and policy basis permits it. The architecture should distinguish inference/RAG, evaluation, academic research, embeddings and training instead of assigning one blanket “AI use” permission.
